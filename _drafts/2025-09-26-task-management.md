I’ve noticed something consistent over the years: most software engineers don’t enjoy task management. And honestly, I don’t blame them. Writing code is creative, technical, and rewarding; updating tickets or moving cards often feels like overhead. Some see it as “real work vs. paperwork.” Others have been burned by heavy, bureaucratic systems where task management seemed to serve the tool instead of the team.

But here’s the paradox: without some kind of task management, even the best engineering teams drift. Tasks get forgotten, priorities clash, deadlines slip. Task management—though not glamorous—is simply the art of knowing what needs to be done, who’s doing it, and when it should be finished.

It doesn’t have to be painful. In fact, when done pragmatically, task management scales smoothly with the team, providing just enough structure to keep everyone aligned without getting in the way.

I’ve lived through this evolution myself, starting with text files and eventually graduating to tools like JIRA. The transitions weren’t about chasing shiny apps; they were about solving real problems as our team grew.

Stage 1: The Humble Text File

When we were just a two-person operation, our “system” was a single shared text file. We listed out what needed doing, checked things off, and moved on. It was quick, frictionless, and exactly what we needed at the time.

There was no point in overcomplicating things. With so few tasks and only two people, context lived in our heads. The text file was just a memory aid.

Stage 2: The Board Appears

When we grew to four or five, the cracks began to show. Suddenly, I’d ask, “Wait—are you on that task, or am I?” or “Is this actually done, or still half-baked?” The text file wasn’t enough anymore.

That’s when we graduated to a shared Kanban board (we used Trello, but anything would have worked). Seeing tasks move from “To Do” to “In Progress” to “Done” gave us visibility we desperately needed. Assigning cards made ownership clear.

It wasn’t fancy—but it solved a real problem.

Stage 3: Growing Pains

Fast forward a bit, and the team hit ten people. Projects overlapped. Deadlines collided. Dependencies snuck up on us. Suddenly, “To Do / Doing / Done” wasn’t enough.

This is when we had to bring in more structured tools: Asana at first, later JIRA when engineering work really demanded it. At first, I resisted—it felt heavy compared to our simple board. But once I saw how epics, stories, and sprints brought order to the chaos, I understood the tradeoff.

The key, though, was restraint. We didn’t turn on every feature at once. We only added structure where pain existed. Reporting dashboards? Useful. Three layers of approvals for a bug fix? Absolutely not.

What I Learned

Looking back, I realized there was a pattern:

Start simple. A checklist or text file works wonders for a small crew.

Upgrade when pain appears. Don’t adopt JIRA just because you might grow someday—wait until you actually feel the need.

Add structure gradually. Complexity should solve problems, not create them.

Task management is not about chasing the “perfect tool.” It’s about giving your team just enough process to stay aligned and effective, without drowning in bureaucracy.

Closing Thought

Whether you’re running a two-person side project or managing a fifty-person engineering team, the principle is the same: grow your task management system alongside your team, not ahead of it. Respect the humble checklist, embrace the big tools when the time comes, and above all—stay pragmatic.
